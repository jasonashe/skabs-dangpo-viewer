# The title page — what the book is a commentary on

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> ༄༅།། བསྟན་བཅོས་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པའི་རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་པ་དང་བཅས་པའི་རྣམ་བཤད་རྣམ་པ་གཉིས་ཀྱི་དཀའ་བའི་གནད་གསལ་བར་བྱེད་པ་ལེགས་བཤད་སྐལ་བཟང་ཀླུ་དབང་གི་རོལ་མཚོ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ལས་སྐབས་དང་པོའི་སྤྱི་དོན་བཞུགས་སོ།།

**Preceding Section:** none. This is the first line of the volume, the
མཚན་བྱང or title page, standing on PDF page 36 before anything else.

**Following Section:** p2, the salutation and pledge to compose. It opens
ན་མོ་གུ་རུ་ཨཱརྱ་མཻ་ཏྲི་ཡེ།, then restates this same title in an *expanded* form
that names the two རྣམ་བཤད outright — ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་གྱི་ཕྲེང་བ and
རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན — before running through the lineage from Śākyamuni and
Maitreya down to Chökyi Gyaltsen's own teachers, and closing with the
composition-pledge verse. p3 then begins the treatise proper with the fourfold
division of the preliminaries.

**How to read a title page.** Nothing is argued here. But a Tibetan title of
this kind is a dense piece of information and it is doing four jobs at once: it
names the **base text** being expounded, it names the **intermediate
commentaries** whose difficulties are to be resolved, it gives the work's
**proper name** in metaphor, and it identifies **which volume of that work**
the reader is holding. Every downstream question about the yig cha's authority
— what it may appeal to, what it must reconcile, whom it answers to — is
already settled by these 186 characters. The one thing a reader must not do is
skip it.

The decisive control on the reading is the composition-pledge verse at the end
of p2, where Chökyi Gyaltsen restates the whole title in his own voice as
something he is about to write:

> [མདོ་རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་བའི་རྣམ་བཤད་རྣམ་གཉིས་ཀྱི། ། དཀའ་བའི་གནད་རྣམས་གསལ་བར་བྱེད་པ་ཡི། ། མདོ་དང་རྩ་འགྲེལ་སྦྱོར་བའི་ཆུ་གཏེར་ཆེ། ། ལེགས་བཤད་ཡིད་བཞིན་ནོར་བུས་རབ་ཏུ་གཏམ། ། སྐལ་བཟང་ཀླུ་དབང་དགའ་བའི་རྣམ་འགྱུར་གྱིས། ། རོལ་པའི་མཚོ་ཆེན་བདག་གིས་བྲི་བར་བྱ། །](quote:p1-q01)
>
> "Clarifying the difficult points of the two full expositions of sūtra,
> Ornament and commentary — a great water-treasury joining the sūtra with the
> root text and its commentary, utterly studded with the wish-fulfilling jewels
> of good explanation, the great ocean in which fortunate nāga-lords sport with
> gestures of delight — this I shall write."

Every element of the title page recurs there, in the same order, with
བདག་གིས་བྲི་བར་བྱ at the end. The title is the author's, not a cataloguer's,
and the verse is its gloss.

## 2 · Translation & Breakdown

<!--line 0-5-->
> **༄༅།།**
>
> [The head-mark opening a Tibetan volume.]

<!--line 5-38-->
> **བསྟན་བཅོས་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པའི་རྒྱན་**
>
> The treatise *Ornament for Clear Realizations* —

The root text names itself, in Tibetan translation,
[ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པའི་མན་ངག་གི་བསྟན་བཅོས་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པའི་རྒྱན་ཞེས་བྱ་བའི་ཚིག་ལེའུར་བྱས་པ།](quote:p1-q07),
"the versified treatise called *Ornament for Clear Realizations, the Instruction
on the Perfection of Wisdom*." The title page abbreviates to the last three
words plus བསྟན་བཅོས.

<!--line 38-57-->
> **འགྲེལ་པ་དང་བཅས་པའི་**
>
> — together with its commentary;

Not "commentaries" in general. འགྲེལ་པ with the definite force it has here is
Haribhadra's short commentary, which titles itself simply
[ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པའི་མན་ངག་གི་བསྟན་བཅོས་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པའི་རྒྱན་ཞེས་བྱ་བའི་འགྲེལ་པ།](quote:p1-q08)
— *the* commentary on the Ornament — and is known in the yig cha as the
འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ།.

<!--line 57-80-->
> **རྣམ་བཤད་རྣམ་པ་གཉིས་ཀྱི་**
>
> of the two full expositions [of that] —

<!--line 80-106-->
> **དཀའ་བའི་གནད་གསལ་བར་བྱེད་པ་**
>
> that which clarifies the difficult points;

<!--line 106-142-->
> **ལེགས་བཤད་སྐལ་བཟང་ཀླུ་དབང་གི་རོལ་མཚོ་**
>
> *The Ocean of Play of Fortunate Nāga-Lords, of Good Explanation* —

The compound is elliptical and the pledge verse supplies what is missing: the
ocean is *studded with* the wish-fulfilling jewels of good explanation
(ལེགས་བཤད་ཡིད་བཞིན་ནོར་བུ), and the fortunate nāga-lords are those who sport in
it. Read without the verse, ལེགས་བཤད་ looks like a bare honorific prefix; read
with it, it names what the ocean contains.

<!--line 142-154-->
> **ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ལས་**
>
> — from the [work] so called,

<!--line 154-186-->
> **སྐབས་དང་པོའི་སྤྱི་དོན་བཞུགས་སོ།།**
>
> the *General Meaning of the First Occasion* is here contained.

The ལས is the load-bearing word in the last third. It says this volume is an
*extract from* a larger whole: the reader holds one part of
ལེགས་བཤད་སྐལ་བཟང་ཀླུ་དབང་གི་རོལ་མཚོ, not the thing itself.

*A note on the witnesses.* Of the volumes in this corpus that carry the formula,
one prints དཀའ་བའི་**གནས**་ where the rest print དཀའ་བའི་**གནད**་. In a corpus
recovered by OCR a ད/ས difference is not evidence of a real variant, and nothing
in the sense turns on it; the pledge verse in p2 has གནད.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

- **Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** none. A title page has no counterpart in
  Maitreya's verses. What it does is *name* them: the root text's own Tibetan
  title-line ([q07](quote:p1-q07)) is what the opening words abbreviate.
- **Haribhadra's commentary location:** none, for the same reason. Haribhadra's
  འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ། is named, not commented on — it is one storey down in the
  structure this title erects, and the title reaches it only through the two
  Tibetan expositions.
- **Text location:** outside the yig cha's own outline. p3 opens the
  dkar chag proper with the two-fold division into
  བསྟན་བཅོས་ཀྱི་སྔོན་དུ་འགྲོ་བ and
  སྐབས་སུ་བབས་པའི་བསྟན་བཅོས་དངོས་བཤད་པ; p1 and p2 stand before that division,
  as title and salutation. They are frame, not content.
- **Section type:** paratext — མཚན་བྱང, the title page, with the
  ལས…བཞུགས་སོ formula that identifies a volume within a collection.
- **Argumentative role:** it fixes the work's *terms of reference*. Because the
  title names two expositions rather than one, everything the yig cha does
  downstream — every place it must ask whether Tsongkhapa and Gyaltsab Je are
  saying the same thing — is licensed here and nowhere else.

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**Core meaning: a three-storey structure.** Parse the genitives and the title
describes a building. The ground floor is Indian: the *Ornament*
**together with its commentary** (རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་པ་དང་བཅས་པ), Maitreya plus
Haribhadra, treated as a single object of exposition. The first floor is Gelug:
the **two full expositions** of that object. The second floor is this book,
which does not expound the Indian material directly but **clarifies the
difficult points of the two expositions**. A Sera Jey monk should notice what
this rules out. The yig cha does not present itself as a commentary on the
*Ornament*. It presents itself as a commentary on two commentaries, and its
proper business is the places where those two are hard.

**Which two.** The title does not say, and p2 does. The first is Tsongkhapa's,
whose own title page reads
[ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པའི་མན་ངག་གི་བསྟན་བཅོས་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པའི་རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་པ་དང་བཅས་པའི་རྒྱ་ཆེར་བཤད་པའི་ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་གྱི་ཕྲེང་བ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་བཞུགས་སོ། །](quote:p1-q05).
Note that the phrase རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་པ་དང་བཅས་པ is Tsongkhapa's, taken over into
p1 word for word. Chökyi Gyaltsen is not describing his base texts in his own
vocabulary; he is quoting the *gser phreng*'s description of *its* base text and
building one storey on top of it.

The second is Gyaltsab Je's, whose title page reads
[ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པའི་མན་ངག་གི་བསྟན་བཅོས་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པའི་རྒྱན་གྱི་འགྲེལ་བ་དོན་གསལ་བའི་རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན་ཅེས་བྱ་བ་བཞུགས་སོ། །](quote:p1-q06).
This one settles a question the title page leaves open. Gyaltsab's work calls
itself a རྣམ་བཤད **on the འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ**, not on the *Ornament* — which is
why p1 can say "the *Ornament* together with its commentary" and mean one thing
that both expositions take as their base. Both masters, writing within the same
Gelug transmission and both students of Tsongkhapa's own house, are commenting
on the same composite Indian object.

**Where the title itself comes from.** The wording of p1 is not invented. Ngok
Lotsāwa Loden Sherab's Prajñāpāramitā treatise announces itself as

> [ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པའི་མན་ངག་གི་བསྟན་བཅོས་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པའི་རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་པ་དང་བཅས་པའི་དཀའ་བའི་གནས་རྣམས་རྣམ་པར་བཤད་ནས་རང་གཞན་གྱི་གྲུབ་པའི་མཐའ་རྣམ་པར་དབྱེ་བ་ལུང་རིགས་ཀྱི་རོལ་མཚོ་བཞུགས་སོ།།](quote:p1-q04)
>
> "Having fully explained the difficult points of the *Ornament together with
> its commentary*, distinguishing our own and others' tenets — *The Ocean of
> Play of Scripture and Reasoning*."

Three of p1's four structural elements are already there: the same base object
in the same words (རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་པ་དང་བཅས་པ), the same task
(དཀའ་བའི་གནས་རྣམས་རྣམ་པར་བཤད་པ), and the same image for the proper name
(རོལ་མཚོ). Only the middle storey is new — Ngok explains the difficulties of
the Indian pair directly; Chökyi Gyaltsen interposes the two Gelug expositions
and explains *their* difficulties. Set the two titles side by side and the
history of the genre is legible in a single line: what Ngok did for the Indian
text in the eleventh century, the Sera Jey yig cha does for the Tibetan
expositions of it.

This is also why p2's homage to Ngok is not a formality. In the middle of a
lineage list that gives everyone else a half-line, Ngok and his nephew get a
whole verse to themselves, prefaced ཁྱད་པར — "in particular" — and called
ལུགས་འདིར་བཀའ་དྲིན་མཉམ་མེད་པ, "unequalled in kindness *in this system*." The
title page and the salutation are making the same acknowledgement, one in
borrowed words and the other in open ones.

**A series title, not a book title.** The words of p1 are not peculiar to this
volume. The same formula, character for character down to the ལས, heads other
volumes of Chökyi Gyaltsen's Prajñāpāramitā curriculum — for instance
[བསྟན་བཅོས་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པའི་རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་པ་དང་བཅས་པའི་རྣམ་བཤད་རྣམ་པ་གཉིས་ཀྱི་དཀའ་བའི་གནད་གསལ་བར་བྱེད་པ་ལེགས་བཤད་སྐལ་བཟང་ཀླུ་དབང་གི་རོལ་མཚོ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ལས་བསམ་གཟུགས་སྤྱི་དོན་བཞུགས་སོ། །](quote:p1-q02),
the same title issuing in the བསམ་གཟུགས་སྤྱི་དོན།. Two things follow. First, the
proper name ལེགས་བཤད་སྐལ་བཟང་ཀླུ་དབང་གི་རོལ་མཚོ belongs to the whole
phar phyin spyi don, of which སྐབས་དང་པོའི་སྤྱི་དོན is the opening instalment —
which is what ལས was saying. Second, the umbrella reaches past the eight
skabs of the *Ornament* to the བསམ་གཟུགས material as well: the series is the
Sera Jey phar phyin course, not a running commentary on one text.

A dedicatory verse standing at a volume-break in the same collection names the
work by its short title and glosses it independently:

> [ཤེར་ཕྱིན་སྦས་དོན་རིན་ཆེན་གསལ་བའི་གཏེར། ། མདོ་རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་གསུམ་སྦྱར་བའི་ལེགས་བཤད་ཆེ། ། ཀླུ་དབང་རོལ་མཚོ་རྒྱས་བྱེད་སྦྱོར་བརྩོན་དགེས། ། རྨད་བྱུང་བཤད་སྒྲུབ་དགའ་སྟོན་རྒྱས་གྱུར་ཅིག](quote:p1-q03)
>
> "The treasury that reveals the jewel of the Prajñāpāramitā's hidden meaning;
> the great good explanation that joins the three — sūtra, Ornament and
> commentary; may the effort that expands the *Nāga-Lords' Ocean of Play* …
> spread the marvellous festival of exposition and practice."

The verse is unattributed in the text as it stands, so it should not be treated
as Chökyi Gyaltsen's own; but it is independent testimony that ཀླུ་དབང་རོལ་མཚོ
circulated as the short title, and its gloss མདོ་རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་གསུམ་སྦྱར་བ,
"joining the three," is the same reading as the pledge verse's
མདོ་དང་རྩ་འགྲེལ་སྦྱོར་བ.

**The metaphor.** The immediate picture is a nāga's ocean-palace: nāgas dwell in
water, hold jewels, and are proverbially delighted by them. The book is the
ocean, its good explanations are the jewels strewn through it, and the
"fortunate nāga-lords" are the students fit to enjoy them. There is a second
resonance available to any Prajñāpāramitā reader, and it is worth saying that
the title does *not* itself make the link — this is a reading, not a claim the
text supports. Tsongkhapa, narrating Nāgārjuna's life in the *gser phreng*,
says he
[ཀླུའི་ཡུལ་ནས་ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པ་ལ་སོགས་པའི་མདོ་རྣམས་སྤྱན་དྲངས་ནས](quote:p1-q16),
"invited the Prajñāpāramitā sūtras and the rest from the realm of the nāgas."
The nāgas are the Mother's custodians. To call the fit student a
ཀླུ་དབང་ is to seat him where the sūtras were kept.

**Historical debate.** There is no doctrinal dispute in a title page, and it
would be false to manufacture one. What there is instead is a disagreement about
*base texts*, visible only when this title is set beside its Drepung
counterpart; that belongs to section 5.

**The making of the book.** For anything about how this volume came to be
written, the evidence is a single tier-5 witness — the hagiography by his
student Panchen Delek Nyima — and that should be said before it is used. Within
those limits it is unusually apposite. The biographer describes the training
that produced this genre:

> [མངོན་རྟོགས་རྒྱན་རྩ་འགྲེལ། མངོན་པ་མཛོད། དབུ་མ་ལ་འཇུག་པ་རྣམས་ཀྱི་བརྒལ་བརྟག་སྤྱི་དོན་རྒྱལ་བ་ཙོང་ཁ་པ་ཡབ་སྲས་རྣམས་ཀྱི་ཊཱིཀྐའི་སྟེང་ནས་གཞུང་ཚིག་གི་འབྲུ་གནོན། དཀའ་བའི་གནས་རྣམས་ལ་དོགས་གཅོད་རྣམས་ལུང་རིགས་ཀྱི་སྒོ་ནས་མཐའ་ཆོད་པ](quote:p1-q11)
>
> "the debate-analysis and general meaning of the *Ornament* root-and-commentary,
> the *Abhidharmakośa* and the *Madhyamakāvatāra* — pressing out the words of
> the text on the basis of the ṭīkās of Tsongkhapa and his sons, settling the
> doubts on the difficult points by scripture and reasoning."

That is p1's title as a curriculum: a སྤྱི་དོན, worked over the Gelug ṭīkās,
aimed at the དཀའ་བའི་གནས. The hagiography also reports a dream in which Gyaltsab
Je himself
[རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན་གྱི་དཀའ་གནད་མང་པོ་ཞིབ་ཏུ་གནང་ནས](quote:p1-q12),
"gave in detail many of the difficult points of the *Rnam bshad snying po'i
rgyan*," after which he retained most of the exposition on waking. Whatever a
reader makes of the vision, the *phrase* is the title's own — the second of the
two expositions, and its dka' gnad.

**What the biographer says is missing.** In the catalogue of Chökyi Gyaltsen's
writings the same source records
[ཕར་ཕྱིན་གྱི་སྤྱི་དོན་སྐབས་དང་པོའི་འཕྲོ་ཅུང་ཟད་མ་གཏོགས་ཆ་ཚང་བ](quote:p1-q13)
— "the Phar phyin spyi don, complete except for a small remaining portion of
the first skabs." The one volume he singles out as not entirely finished is the
one this title page heads. What exactly was left, and whether the text as
received here includes it or was completed by another hand, cannot be settled
from a title page, and I do not know the answer; it is worth carrying as an open
question when a later paragraph of this volume reads unlike its neighbours. Note
also that the catalogue entry says ཕར་ཕྱིན་གྱི་སྤྱི་དོན and not
ཀླུ་དབང་གི་རོལ་མཚོ — the biographer names the work by genre and subject, the
title page by its proper name.

**A note on the evidence.** The reading of the title itself rests on tier-1
material and is secure: the pledge verse ([q01](quote:p1-q01)) is Chökyi
Gyaltsen's own gloss on his own title, and the parallel volume-heading
([q02](quote:p1-q02)) is the same author's. The identification of the two
expositions rests on tier-2 title pages, which is as good as such an
identification gets. Everything about *composition history* rests on tier 5 and
should be held more loosely than the rest of this analysis.

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

**What is disputed?** Not a doctrine. What is at issue is the **base text of the
yig cha** — which Gelug exposition, or expositions, a Prajñāpāramitā curriculum
takes as the thing it explains. That question is invisible until two title pages
are put side by side, and then it is unmistakable.

**Who argues what?**

- **Sera Jey — Jetsun Chökyi Gyaltsen.** *Two.* The title reads
  རྣམ་བཤད་རྣམ་པ་གཉིས་ཀྱི་དཀའ་བའི་གནད་གསལ་བར་བྱེད་པ, and p2 names them as
  Tsongkhapa's *gser phreng* and Gyaltsab Je's *snying po'i rgyan*.
- **Drepung Loseling — Panchen Sonam Drakpa.** *One.* His yig cha's title page
  opens with the identical formula and then diverges at exactly the point that
  matters:
  [བསྟན་བཅོས་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པའི་རྒྱན་འགྲེལ་པ་དང་བཅས་པའི་རྣམ་བཤད་སྙིང་པོའི་རྒྱན་གྱི་དགོངས། འགྲེལ་ཚིག་དོན་རབ་གསལ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་བཞུགས་སོ། །](quote:p1-q09)
  — "clarifying the intent **of the *Rnam bshad snying po'i rgyan*** on the
  Ornament together with its commentary." Same ground floor, same first phrase;
  one exposition instead of two, and an འགྲེལ་ཚིག, a word-commentary, rather
  than a སྤྱི་དོན.

- **The eleventh-century precedent — Ngok Lotsāwa.** *Neither.* His
  ལུང་རིགས་ཀྱི་རོལ་མཚོ ([q04](quote:p1-q04)) takes the difficult points of the
  Indian pair themselves and adds a tenet-classification. He is not a rival to
  either yig cha; he is the form both of them inherited, and the reason the
  question can be posed as "which exposition" at all is that the Gelug
  expositions had by then interposed themselves between the reader and the
  Indian text.

The parallel is close enough that the divergence is deliberate on somebody's
part, and a debater should be careful about which way the borrowing runs: the
relative dating of the two yig chas is not something this title page decides,
and I will not guess at it.

**Why does it matter?** Because it determines what counts as an *objection*.
A curriculum built on Gyaltsab alone can treat his words as the standard and ask
only what they mean. A curriculum built on Tsongkhapa *and* Gyaltsab has a
further duty whenever the two do not obviously agree: it must say how they are
to be read together, or which is decisive. That duty is the standing occupation
of a spyi don, and it is written into the Sera Jey title in the single word
གཉིས. It is also why the Sera Jey text so often reaches for the *gser phreng*
where a Loseling text need not.

**A third exposition, not counted.** Khedrup Je wrote a
[ཤེས་རབ་ཀྱི་ཕ་རོལ་ཏུ་ཕྱིན་པའི་མན་ངག་གི་བསྟན་བཅོས་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པའི་རྒྱན་གྱི་འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ་བའི་རྣམ་བཤད་རྟོགས་པར་དཀའ་བའི་སྣང་བ་བཞུགས་སོ། །](quote:p1-q10)
on exactly the same base — a རྣམ་བཤད on the འགྲེལ་པ་དོན་གསལ, by the other of
Tsongkhapa's two chief disciples, and one that announces in its very name that
it is about what is *hard to realize*. The title page counts two, not three. The
hagiography, meanwhile, describes Chökyi Gyaltsen as trained on the ṭīkās of
ཙོང་ཁ་པ་ཡབ་སྲས, "Tsongkhapa father and sons," a phrase that ordinarily takes in
Khedrup as well ([q11](quote:p1-q11)). So the number two is a selection, not an
inventory of what was available. I do not know from this passage why it was
made, and the title page gives no reason; the honest thing is to flag it and let
the yig cha's actual citation practice, paragraph by paragraph, answer the
question.

**Resolution.** For Sera Jey the two-exposition base stands, and it is
constitutive rather than incidental: it makes this yig cha a work of
*reconciliation* between the Golden Rosary and the Ornament of the Essence, not
a gloss on either. That is the sense in which p1 is not merely a label.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **སྤྱི་དོན།** — "general meaning": the genre this volume belongs to.
  - **How understood here:** not a summary and not a running gloss, but a
    topic-by-topic treatment of the *difficult points* of a base text, organised
    by issue rather than by lemma. The biographer's description of Chökyi
    Gyaltsen teaching — [གྲྭ་པ་མང་པོ་ལ་གཞུང་སོ་སོའི་ཊཱིཀ་དང་། དཀའ་གནད་རྣམས་ལ་མཐའ་དཔྱོད་སྤྱི་དོན་གནང་བའི་ཚེ](quote:p1-q14),
    "giving many monks the ṭīkā of each text, and mtha' dpyod and spyi don on
    the difficult points" — puts the genre exactly where the title puts it: the
    ṭīkā carries the words, the spyi don and mtha' dpyod carry the dka' gnad.
  - **Debates:** the boundary between སྤྱི་དོན and མཐའ་དཔྱོད is drawn by
    practice rather than definition, and the two volumes on this same skabs do
    not divide the material the same way. It is not safe to assume a topic
    absent from one is absent from the other.

- **མཐའ་དཔྱོད།** — the companion genre, "analysis to the limit."
  - **How understood here:** relevant to p1 by contrast. The companion volume on
    this same first skabs carries a wholly different title and describes itself
    as
    [སྐབས་དང་པོའི་མཐའ་དཔྱོད་ལེགས་བཤད་གསེར་གྱི་ཕྲེང་བ་མཁས་པའི་མགུལ་རྒྱན་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་རྗེ་བཙུན་ཆོས་ཀྱི་རྒྱལ་མཚན་དཔལ་བཟང་པོའི་གསུང་ཟིན་བྲིས་སུ](quote:p1-q15)
    — "Jetsun Chökyi Gyaltsen's *speech*, set down in notes." The spyi don
    carries a unified authorial series title and a first-person pledge to write;
    the mtha' dpyod presents itself as a record of teaching. Where the two
    disagree, that difference in status is worth remembering before deciding
    which to prefer.

- **རྣམ་བཤད།** — full exposition.
  - **How understood here:** used in p1 as a *countable* noun —
    "two རྣམ་བཤད" — and so functioning as a class of works rather than a
    description of a method. Of the two intended, only Gyaltsab's actually
    carries the word in its title; Tsongkhapa's calls itself a
    རྒྱ་ཆེར་བཤད་པ. The title page treats the two as one class anyway, which is
    itself the claim that they are commensurable.

- **དཀའ་བའི་གནད།** — the difficult points; the yig cha's stated object.
  - **How understood here:** the difficulties are located in the *expositions*,
    not in the *Ornament*. This is a precise claim and a limiting one: where the
    two Gelug expositions are clear, a spyi don has, on its own account, nothing
    to add.
  - **Debates:** the same phrase recurs across the tradition for what a student
    brings to a teacher and what a teacher resolves — the hagiography uses it
    both of Chökyi Gyaltsen receiving ([q12](quote:p1-q12)) and of his giving
    ([q14](quote:p1-q14)). Its use in a title is therefore also a claim about
    the book's function in a monastery, not only about its contents.

- **འགྲེལ་པ་དང་བཅས་པ།** — "together with the commentary."
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** the formula answers to the Indian pairing of
    *kārikā* with *vṛtti* — Haribhadra's short commentary is a
    *Sphuṭārthā vṛtti*, and its Tibetan colophon-title accordingly reads
    འགྲེལ་པ.
  - **How understood here:** a frozen expression naming a single composite
    object, root plus Haribhadra, inherited from the *gser phreng*'s own title
    ([q05](quote:p1-q05)). When later paragraphs say རྩ་འགྲེལ they mean this
    same pair.

- **ཀླུ་དབང་།** — nāga-lord.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *nāgendra* / *nāgeśvara*.
  - **How understood here:** the reader, not the author. The fortunate
    (སྐལ་བཟང) student is the one at home in this ocean. The self-effacing move
    is worth noticing: the author claims to have built the ocean, not to be its
    lord.
  - **Debates:** none textual. The further association with the nāgas as
    custodians of the Prajñāpāramitā, from whom Nāgārjuna retrieved it
    ([q16](quote:p1-q16)), is a resonance the title leaves unstated, and I offer
    it as a reading rather than as something the corpus asserts of this title.
