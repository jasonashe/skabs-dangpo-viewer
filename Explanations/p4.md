# Seventeen, then nine: two counts of the root sūtra refused

## 1 · Passage & Context

**Source Text:**

> དེ་རྣམས་ཀྱི་ནང་ནས་འདིའི་བཤད་བྱ་རྩ་བའི་མདོ་ལ་སྔ་རབས་པ་ཁ་ཅིག ། དེ་ལ་ཡུམ་སྲས་བཅུ་བདུན་དུ་ཡོད་དེ། ཡུམ་དྲུག ། སྲས་བཅུ་གཅིག་ཏུ་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར། … ཞེས་ཟེར་ན། ཤེར་ཕྱིན་གྱི་མདོ་ལ་བཅུ་བདུན་གྱིས་གྲངས་ངེས་བྱེད་པ་མི་འཐད་དེ། དེ་གྲངས་ཉུང་བའི་སྐྱོན་ཡང་ཡོད། དེ་གྲངས་མང་བའི་སྐྱོན་ཡང་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར། … ཨར་ཆེན་པོ་དང་ཕྱིས་ཀྱི་མཁས་པ་ཁ་ཅིག ། ལུགས་དེ་བཀག་ནས་དེ་ལ་དགུར་ཡོད་དེ། … ཞེས་ཟེར་བ་རིགས་པ་སྔ་མས་ཁེགས་སོ། ། ཁོ་ན་རེ། མདོ་སྡུད་པ་ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པའི་ནང་ཚན་དུ་གསུངས་པ་ལོ་ཙྪ་བ་རྣམས་ཀྱིས་གླེགས་བམ་བོད་དུ་བསྒྱུར་བའི་ཚེ་བཀོད་པ་ཡིན་གྱི། … ཟེར་ན་མི་འཐད་དེ།

(The two lists and the closing arguments are elided here; section 2 gives all
of it.)

**Preceding Section:** p3, which set out the four preliminaries and, opening the
first of them, established that the root sūtra of the *Ornament* is the
Prajñāpāramitā, proving it from the Śāriputra passage and from the entrustment
chapter. Its last words are ཞེས་གསུངས་པའི་ཕྱིར།; p4 begins
དེ་རྣམས་ཀྱི་ནང་ནས — "among those."

**Following Section:** p5, the རང་ལུགས: the three Mothers — extensive, middling
and condensed — are the root sūtra, on the authority of Haribhadra's
འགྲེལ་པ (རྒྱལ་བའི་ཡུམ་རྣམ་པ་གསུམ་ཆར་ལས་ཀྱང་བརྗོད་པར་བྱ་བ་ཉིད་དུ་བསྟན་པ) and of
a verse about how the learned condense the meaning from all the Mothers.

**What this paragraph is.** Ground-clearing, in two rounds with an appendix.
Round one refutes a count of seventeen; round two refutes a count of nine; the
appendix disposes of an objection that would have rescued the second count.
Nothing positive is asserted. That comes in p5, and this paragraph exists so
that when it comes, only one candidate is left standing.

## 2 · Translation & Breakdown

<!--line 0-61-->
> **དེ་རྣམས་ཀྱི་ནང་ནས་འདིའི་བཤད་བྱ་རྩ་བའི་མདོ་ལ་སྔ་རབས་པ་ཁ་ཅིག ། **
>
> Among those, as to the root sūtra this [*Ornament*] is to expound, some
> earlier Tibetans [say]:

<!--line 61-135-->
> **དེ་ལ་ཡུམ་སྲས་བཅུ་བདུན་དུ་ཡོད་དེ། ཡུམ་དྲུག ། སྲས་བཅུ་གཅིག་ཏུ་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> There are seventeen of them, Mothers and Sons — because there are six Mothers
> and eleven Sons.

<!--line 135-231-->
> **ཡུམ་དྲུག་ནི། འབུམ། ཉི་ཁྲི། ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ། ཤེས་རབ་ཁྲི་པ། བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ། མདོ་སྡུད་པ་རྣམས་ཡིན་ལ།**
>
> The six Mothers are: the Hundred Thousand, the Twenty-five Thousand, the
> Eighteen Thousand, the Ten Thousand, the Eight Thousand, and the
> *Saṃcayagāthā*.

<!--line 231-396-->
> **དེ་རྣམས་ལ་ཡུམ་ཞེས་བརྗོད་པའི་རྒྱུ་མཚན་ཡོད་དེ། དེ་རྣམས་བརྗོད་བྱ་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པ་བརྒྱད་དུ་ཕྱེ་ནས་སྟོན་པའི་ཤེར་ཕྱིན་གྱི་མདོ་ཡིན་པའི་རྒྱུ་མཚན་གྱིས་དེ་ལྟར་བརྗོད་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> There is a reason for calling these Mothers: they are so called because they
> are Prajñāpāramitā sūtras that set out their subject-matter **divided into
> the eight clear realizations**.

<!--line 396-621-->
> **སྲས་བཅུ་གཅིག་ནི། ཤེར་ཕྱིན་བདུན་བརྒྱ་པ། ལྔ་བརྒྱ་པ། སུམ་བརྒྱ་པ། ཚུལ་བརྒྱ་ལྔ་བཅུ་པ། ལྔ་བཅུ་པ། སྒོ་ཉེར་ལྔ་པ། རབ་རྩལ་རྣམ་གནོན་གྱིས་ཞུས་པ། ཀོའུ་ཤི་ཀས་ཞུས་པ། ཤེར་ཕྱིན་ཡི་གེ་ཉུང་ངུ་། ཤེར་ཕྱིན་ཡི་གེ་གཅིག་མ། ཤེས་རབ་སྙིང་པོ་རྣམས་ཡིན་ལ།**
>
> The eleven Sons are: the Seven Hundred, the Five Hundred, the Three Hundred
> [*Vajracchedikā*], the Hundred-and-Fifty Modes, the Fifty, the Twenty-five
> Doors, the *Suvikrāntavikrāmiparipṛcchā*, the *Kauśikapṛcchā*, the
> Prajñāpāramitā in Few Letters, the Prajñāpāramitā in One Letter, and the
> *Heart of Wisdom*.

<!--line 621-820-->
> **དེ་རྣམས་ལ་སྲས་ཞེས་བརྗོད་པའི་རྒྱུ་མཚན་ཡོད་དེ། དེ་རྣམས་བརྗོད་བྱ་མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པ་བརྒྱད་དུ་ཕྱེ་ནས་མི་སྟོན་པར་བརྗོད་བྱ་ཕྱོགས་རེ་བ་ཙམ་སྟོན་པའི་ཤེར་ཕྱིན་གྱི་མདོ་ཡིན་པའི་རྒྱུ་མཚན་གྱིས་དེ་ལྟར་བརྗོད་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> And there is a reason for calling these Sons: they are so called because they
> are Prajñāpāramitā sūtras that do **not** set out their subject-matter
> divided into the eight clear realizations, but show only some one portion of
> it.

<!--line 820-959-->
> **ཞེས་ཟེར་ན། ཤེར་ཕྱིན་གྱི་མདོ་ལ་བཅུ་བདུན་གྱིས་གྲངས་ངེས་བྱེད་པ་མི་འཐད་དེ། དེ་གྲངས་ཉུང་བའི་སྐྱོན་ཡང་ཡོད། དེ་གྲངས་མང་བའི་སྐྱོན་ཡང་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> — so they say. Fixing the number of Prajñāpāramitā sūtras at seventeen is
> untenable, because it has the fault of being too few and also the fault of
> being too many.

Both faults at once, of the same count. That is the shape of the refutation:
the enumeration is not merely inaccurate but incoherent, since what is left out
and what is double-counted are established by two different kinds of evidence.

<!--line 959-1099-->
> **དང་པོ་གྲུབ་སྟེ༑ ཤེར་ཕྱིན་ཉི་མའི་སྙིང་པོ། ཟླ་བའི་སྙིང་པོ། ཀུན་ཏུ་བཟང་པོ། ལག་ན་རྡོ་རྗེ། རྡོ་རྗེ་རྒྱལ་མཚན་གྱིས་ཞུས་པ་རྣམས་དེར་མ་འདུས་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> The first is established, because the *Sūryagarbha*, the *Candragarbha*, the
> *Samantabhadra*, the *Vajrapāṇi* and the *Vajraketu-paripṛcchā*
> Prajñāpāramitās are not included in it.

<!--line 1099-1247-->
> **གཉིས་པ་གྲུབ་སྟེ། ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ་ལ་ལེའུ་གྱ་བདུན་ཡོད་པའི་མདོ་སྡུད་པ་དེའི་ལེའུ་གྱ་བཞི་པ་ཡིན་པས། དེ་ཟུར་པར་འདྲེན་ན་ལེའུ་གཞན་རྣམས་ལ་ཡང་མཚུངས་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> The second is established, because the Eighteen Thousand has eighty-seven
> chapters and the *Saṃcayagāthā* is its eighty-fourth; so if that is drawn out
> as a separate item, the same holds for the other chapters too.

<!--line 1247-1316-->
> **ཨར་ཆེན་པོ་དང་ཕྱིས་ཀྱི་མཁས་པ་ཁ་ཅིག ། ལུགས་དེ་བཀག་ནས་དེ་ལ་དགུར་ཡོད་དེ།**
>
> Great Ar and some later scholars, having refuted that system, [say] there are
> nine:

<!--line 1316-1652-->
> **རྒྱས་པའི་རྒྱས་པ་ཤེར་ཕྱིན་བམ་པོ་ལྔ་བརྒྱ་པ། རྒྱས་པའི་འབྲིང་བམ་པོ་བཞི་བརྒྱ་པ། རྒྱས་པའི་བསྡུས་པ་སྟོང་ཕྲག་བརྒྱ་པ། འབྲིང་གི་རྒྱས་པ་སྟོང་ཕྲག་ཉི་ཤུ་ལྔ་པ། འབྲིང་གི་འབྲིང་ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ། འབྲིང་གི་བསྡུས་པ་ཤེས་རབ་ཁྲི་པ། བསྡུས་པའི་རྒྱས་པ་ཤེར་ཕྱིན་བམ་པོ་བཅོ་བརྒྱད་པ༑ བསྡུས་པའི་འབྲིང་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ། བསྡུས་པའི་བསྡུས་པ་མདོ་སྡུད་པ་རྣམས་སུ་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> — because there are: extensive-of-the-extensive, the Prajñāpāramitā in five
> hundred bam po; extensive-of-the-middling, in four hundred bam po;
> extensive-of-the-condensed, the Hundred Thousand; middling-of-the-extensive,
> the Twenty-five Thousand; middling-of-the-middling, the Eighteen Thousand;
> middling-of-the-condensed, the Ten Thousand; condensed-of-the-extensive, the
> Prajñāpāramitā in eighteen bam po; condensed-of-the-middling, the Eight
> Thousand; condensed-of-the-condensed, the *Saṃcayagāthā*.

<!--line 1652-1695-->
> **ཞེས་ཟེར་བ་རིགས་པ་སྔ་མས་ཁེགས་སོ། ། ཁོ་ན་རེ།**
>
> — so they say. That is refuted by the previous reasoning. [An objector] says:

<!--line 1695-1844-->
> **མདོ་སྡུད་པ་ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པའི་ནང་ཚན་དུ་གསུངས་པ་ལོ་ཙྪ་བ་རྣམས་ཀྱིས་གླེགས་བམ་བོད་དུ་བསྒྱུར་བའི་ཚེ་བཀོད་པ་ཡིན་གྱི། དེ་ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པའི་ནང་ཚན་མ་ཡིན་ཏེ།**
>
> That the *Saṃcayagāthā* is spoken as a member of the Eighteen Thousand is
> something the translators arranged when they turned the volume into Tibetan;
> it is not a member of the Eighteen Thousand. Because:

<!--line 1844-1990-->
> **བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཀྱིས་འཁོར་བཞི་པོ་དེ་དག་ཡང་དག་པར་རབ་ཏུ་དགའ་བར་མཛད་པའི་ཕྱིར། ཞེས་གསུངས་པའི་ཐད་ཀྱི་སྡུད་འགྲེལ་ལས། རྒྱས་པར་བསྟན་ཟིན་པའི་རྗེས་དེ་མ་ཐག་ནས།**
>
> at the place where it says "because the Blessed One caused those four
> assemblies to rejoice fully," the commentary on the *Saṃcayagāthā* says
> "immediately after the extensive teaching had been completed" —

<!--line 1990-2092-->
> **ཞེས་ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ་ལ་སོགས་པ་གསུངས་ཟིན་པའི་རྗེས་སུ་མདོ་སྡུད་པ་གསུངས་པར་བཤད་པའི་ཕྱིར་ཟེར་ན་མི་འཐད་དེ།**
>
> — thus explaining that the *Saṃcayagāthā* was spoken **after** the Eighteen
> Thousand and the rest had been spoken. That is untenable, because:

<!--line 2092-2234-->
> **དེ་ལྟར་ན་ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ་ལ་ཤླཽ་ཀ་ཁྲི་ཚོ་གཅིག་དང་སྟོང་ཕྲག་བརྒྱད་མ་ཚང་བས་ལོ་ཙྪ་བ་རྣམས་ཤླཽ་ཀའི་གྲངས་ཙམ་ལ་ཡང་འཁྲུལ་བར་ཐལ་པའི་སྐྱོན་ཡོད་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> on that account the Eighteen Thousand would fall short of ten thousand plus
> eight thousand ślokas, and so it would follow that the translators were
> mistaken about even the mere count of ślokas — which is a fault.

<!--line 2234-2389-->
> **གཞན་ཡང་༑ བསྡུས་པ་ལ་གསུམ་དུ་འབྱེད་ཚུལ་དེ་མི་འཐད་དེ། མདོ་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ་ལ་བམ་པོ་ཉེར་བཞི་ཡོད་པར་མདོ་དེ་ཉིད་ལས་འབྱུང་བས་སྐབས་འདིར་བམ་ཚད་ལ་མི་མཐུན་པ་མེད་པའི་ཕྱིར།**
>
> Furthermore, that way of dividing the condensed into three is untenable,
> because the Eight Thousand sūtra has twenty-four bam po, as appears in that
> sūtra itself, so that in this context there is no discrepancy about the bam
> po measure.

*Reading note.* This last sentence is elliptical and I give my reading rather
than a certainty. On the ninefold scheme the "condensed-of-the-extensive" is a
Prajñāpāramitā of eighteen bam po and the "condensed-of-the-middling" is the
Eight Thousand; but the Eight Thousand's own colophon gives it twenty-four bam
po, so the member that is supposed to be *larger* is smaller, and a scheme
ordered by bulk collapses. A second reading is possible — that the twenty-four
figure is simply uncontested among the witnesses, so the objector cannot escape
by disputing it — and the two readings support the same conclusion.

## 3 · Structural Context (Dkar Chag)

- **Abhisamayālaṃkāra location:** the counting of sūtras has no counterpart in
  the root verses; it is a Tibetan bibliographical question. But the criterion
  the first count uses is Maitreya's:
  ཤེས་རབ་ཕ་རོལ་ཕྱིན་པ་ནི། ། དངོས་པོ་བརྒྱད་ཀྱིས་ཡང་དག་བཤད། Mother and Son are
  distinguished precisely by whether the eight subjects are
  ཕྱེ་ནས་སྟོན་པ or not, and that is the root text's own line applied as a test.
- **Haribhadra's commentary location:** no counting there either. But
  Haribhadra is directly implicated: the objection at the end of the paragraph
  turns on the སྡུད་པའི་དཀའ་འགྲེལ, his commentary on the *Saṃcayagāthā*, and it
  is a question about that commentary's first sentences.
- **Text location:** first preliminary, second stage — after p3 has established
  *that* the root sūtra is the Prajñāpāramitā, and before p5 says *which*.
- **Section type:** གཞན་ལུགས་དགག་པ in two rounds, with an embedded
  objection-and-reply (ཁོ་ན་རེ … ཟེར་ན་མི་འཐད་དེ) attached to the second.
- **Argumentative role:** purely eliminative. Its work is to leave p5 with a
  clear field, and its second fault does double duty — the same reasoning that
  kills the seventeen kills the nine, which is why the nine gets no separate
  refutation but only རིགས་པ་སྔ་མས་ཁེགས་སོ.

## 4 · Philosophical Exposition & Commentary

**Core meaning.** Two counts, two kinds of criterion. The seventeen-scheme
sorts by **doctrine**: a Mother sets out the eight abhisamayas divided out; a
Son shows only a portion. The nine-scheme sorts by **bulk**: three sizes of
three sizes. Chökyi Gyaltsen's refutations exploit the difference. Against the
seventeen he uses a bibliographical fact (the *Saṃcayagāthā* is a chapter of the
Eighteen Thousand) and a catalogue fact (five more Prajñāpāramitās exist). The
same bibliographical fact then destroys the nine as well, because the nine
places the *Saṃcayagāthā* and the Eighteen Thousand at opposite ends of its
scale, which cannot be right if one is a chapter of the other.

**The whole argument is Tsongkhapa's.** The gser phreng, four folios into the
first volume, reports the same convention in the same words:
[དེ་ལ་སྤྱིར་ཤེར་ཕྱོགས་ལ་ཡུམ་དྲུག་སྲས་བཅུ་གཅིག་སྟེ་སྲས་ཡུམ་བཅུ་བདུན་ཞེས་སྔ་མ་རྣམས་ཀྱིས་བརྡ་བྱས་པ](quote:p4-q01),
with the same six and eleven and the same Mother/Son criterion. It then reports
the objection p4 uses for its first fault, and endorses it:
[དེ་ལ་ཁ་ཅིག་ཉི་མའི་སྙིང་པོ། ཟླ་བའི་སྙིང་པོ། ཀུན་ཏུ་བཟང་པོ། ལག་ན་རྡོ་རྗེ། རྡོ་རྗེ་རྒྱལ་མཚན་རྣམས་ཀྱང་ཤེར་ཕྱིན་ཡིན་པས་སྲས་ཡུམ་གྱི་དོན་དང་། གྲངས་གཉིས་ཀ་ནོར་ཞེས་སྨྲ་བ་ནི་ལེགས་པར་སྨྲས་པ་ཡིན་ལ།](quote:p4-q02)
— "those who say that, since the *Sūryagarbha* and the rest are also
Prajñāpāramitās, both the sense of Son-and-Mother and the number are wrong,
have spoken well." It supplies the second fault, and with more edge than p4
allows itself:
[ཁྱད་པར་དུ་སྡུད་པ་ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ་དང་སོ་སོར་བགྲང་བ་ནི་གླེགས་བམ་ཙམ་ཡང་མ་མཐོང་བར་སྟོན་པ་ཡིན་ཏེ།](quote:p4-q03)
— to count the *Saṃcayagāthā* separately from the Eighteen Thousand "shows one
has not so much as seen the volume" — and gives the chapter number p4 gives:
[ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ་ལ་ལེའུ་གྱ་བདུན་ཡོད་པའི་གྱ་བཞི་པ་ནི་སྡུད་པ་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར་རོ། །](quote:p4-q04).
And it draws p4's second conclusion in advance:
[དེའི་ཕྱིར་ཁྲི་བརྒྱད་སྟོང་པ་འབྲིང་གི་འབྲིང་དུ་བྱས་ནས་སྡུད་པ་བསྡུས་པའི་བསྡུས་པར་བྱས་པ་ཡང་ནོར་རོ། །](quote:p4-q05)
— "therefore making the Eighteen Thousand the middling-of-the-middling and the
*Saṃcayagāthā* the condensed-of-the-condensed is also an error."

That last sentence is what p4's རིགས་པ་སྔ་མས་ཁེགས་སོ is pointing at, and
knowing it changes how the phrase reads. It is not Chökyi Gyaltsen saying "I
have already refuted this"; it is him saying "Tsongkhapa already refuted this,
in that very sentence."

**What Chökyi Gyaltsen adds.** Three things, and they are the reasons the
paragraph is not merely a précis. (i) He **formalises**: the two objections
become the two limbs of a single consequence — too few and too many — so that
the count is shown incoherent rather than merely wrong. (ii) He **names the
opponent**: ཨར་ཆེན་པོ་དང་ཕྱིས་ཀྱི་མཁས་པ་ཁ་ཅིག. Tsongkhapa attributes the
ninefold scheme to no one. (iii) He **adds the objection and reply** about the
translators, which the gser phreng does not have at all.

**Ar Chenpo in the homage.** The ཨར་ of p2's lineage verse
(འབྲེ་ཨར་གཞོན་ནུ་ཚུལ་ཁྲིམས་ཤེས་རབ་འབུམ) is, on the standard identification, Ar
Byangchub Yeshe, one of Ngok Lotsāwa's principal disciples — the same Ar. I
mark that as my own knowledge of the tradition rather than as something this
corpus establishes; no work of his survives here. But if it is right, then the
scholar whose scheme is refuted in p4 was bowed to in p2, and the paragraph
should be read accordingly. Refuting a position is not withdrawing the homage,
and a yig cha that honoured only those it agreed with would have a very short
lineage.

**The objection about the translators, and what it actually rests on.** The
objector's move is clean: if the *Saṃcayagāthā* is really an independent sūtra
spoken *after* the long ones, then its appearance as chapter 84 is an artifact
of Tibetan book-making, and the second fault evaporates. His evidence is
Haribhadra's commentary. Reading that commentary at the place he cites:

> [དེ་ནས་ཀྱི་སྒྲ་ནི་རྒྱས་པར་བསྟན་པའི་དེ་མ་ཐག་ཏུ་འམ། མན་ངག་མདོར་བསྡུས་ནས་བསྟན་པའི་སྐབས་ཡིན་ནོ། །](quote:p4-q07)
>
> "The word 'then' means immediately after the extensive teaching, **or** the
> occasion of the instruction being taught in condensed form."

Two things follow. First, the words p4 puts in the objector's mouth —
རྒྱས་པར་བསྟན་ཟིན་པའི་རྗེས་དེ་མ་ཐག་ནས — are not Haribhadra's; his are
རྒྱས་པར་བསྟན་པའི་དེ་མ་ཐག་ཏུ. The yig cha is paraphrasing, not quoting, and a
debater who cites the སྡུད་འགྲེལ in these words is citing the yig cha's
paraphrase. Second, and more damaging to the objector, Haribhadra offers the
temporal reading as one of *two* alternatives, the second of which is not
temporal at all. The objector's argument needs the first disjunct alone.

Haribhadra also glosses the four assemblies of the same line:
[འཁོར་བཞི་པོ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་ལ་སོགས་པ་ལ་དེ་དག་གིས་སྔར་གཞུང་རྒྱས་པ་མཉན་པས་སྦྱངས་པའི་ཕྱིར](quote:p4-q08)
— they are addressed because "they had already trained by hearing the extensive
text." That does support the objector's picture of a sequence. It is worth
saying that p4's reply does not engage any of this: it answers with an
arithmetical *reductio* about śloka counts, not with a reading of Haribhadra.
The reply is effective but it is not an interpretation of the evidence the
objection rests on, and a careful reader should notice the mismatch.

**The same citation elsewhere in Chökyi Gyaltsen.** The companion volume uses
it too, in a quite different debate — whether the Teacher turned the three
wheels simultaneously:
[བཅོམ་ལྡན་འདས་ཀྱིས་འཁོར་བཞི་པོ་དེ་དག་རབ་ཏུ་དགའ་བར་མཛད་པའི་ཕྱིར། ཞེས་པའི་ཐད་ཀྱི་སྡུད་འགྲེལ་ལས། རྒྱས་པར་བསྟན་ཟིན་པའི་རྗེས་དེ་མ་ཐག་ཏུ།](quote:p4-q09)
There the consequence drawn is that if the three Mothers were taught at once,
this line of the སྡུད་འགྲེལ would be untenable. So Chökyi Gyaltsen himself
treats the passage as establishing a *temporal* sequence — which is exactly what
the objector here needs. He does not deny the reading; he denies the inference
from it.

**A note on the evidence.** Everything load-bearing in this paragraph is tier 2
and tier 3, and it is abundant. What is *not* corroborated anywhere in this
corpus is the arithmetic: the eighteen thousand ślokas, the twenty-four bam po
of the Eight Thousand, the five hundred and four hundred bam po recensions.
Those figures occur here and nowhere else in these forty-seven works. They may
well be right; they are simply not checkable from what is to hand, and a
debater who leans on them should know that.

## 5 · Bones of Contention 🦴

**What is disputed?** How many Prajñāpāramitā sūtras are the root sūtra of the
*Ornament* — and, underneath that, whether the answer is fixed by doctrine or
by bibliography.

**Who argues what?**

- **སྔ་རབས་པ་ཁ་ཅིག, earlier Tibetans:** seventeen, six Mothers and eleven Sons,
  sorted by whether the eight abhisamayas are set out divided.
- **ཨར་ཆེན་པོ and some later scholars:** nine, sorted by bulk in a
  three-by-three of extensive, middling and condensed. Note what this
  presupposes: two Prajñāpāramitās larger than the Hundred Thousand, of five
  hundred and four hundred bam po, identified by measure alone and by no
  transmitted Tibetan text in this corpus.
- **An unnamed objector (ཁོ་ན་རེ):** whatever the count, the *Saṃcayagāthā* is
  independent; its place inside the Eighteen Thousand is the translators' doing.
- **Je Tsongkhapa:** the criterion is neither doctrine-of-eight nor bulk but
  matchability —
  [རྒྱན་འདིས་བསྟན་པའི་མངོན་རྟོགས་ཀྱི་རིམ་པ་རྣམས་དང་སྦྱར་རུང་ཡོད་པའི་མདོ་རྣམས་ནི་འདིའི་རྩ་བའི་མདོ་ཡིན་ལ](quote:p4-q06),
  "the sūtras that admit of being matched with the stages of clear realization
  this *Ornament* teaches are its root sūtras" — and, counting the Indian
  commentaries that actually perform such matching, he concludes that the root
  sūtras are **four or five**.
- **Drepung Gomang — Jamyang Zhepa:** three. He gets there without refuting any
  count, straight from Haribhadra's maṅgala:
  [ཡུམ་རྒྱས་འབྲིང་བསྡུས་གསུམ་ཀུན་གྱི་གསལ་བྱེད་ཀྱི་རྒྱན་དུ་འགྲེལ་པའི་ཆེད་ཡིན་པའི་ཕྱིར](quote:p4-q10)
  — the kārikās exist in order to comment as the ornament that illuminates all
  three Mothers.
- **Sera Jey — Jetsun Chökyi Gyaltsen (p5):** three, ཡུམ་རྒྱས་འབྲིང་བསྡུས.

**Why does it matter?** Because "the sūtra says" is a move in debate, and its
force depends on which sūtras are the root text. If the *Heart Sūtra* is a Son
and not a root sūtra, an argument from the *Heart Sūtra* does not settle a
question about the *Ornament*'s subject-matter; it is at best corroborative. The
count is a rule about what counts as evidence.

**The bone the paragraph does not pick.** Tsongkhapa arrives at four or five;
this yig cha will arrive at three. That is a real divergence between the text
and one of the two expositions the whole book exists to clarify, and p4 does not
mention it — it borrows Tsongkhapa's demolition and stops before his
construction. Whether the difference is substantive or merely a difference of
what one is counting (root sūtras *simpliciter*, against Mothers) is not
settled here. p5 is where it has to be faced, and the honest thing at this point
is to note that the paragraph has used the gser phreng selectively and to say so
rather than to let the borrowing look total.

**Resolution.** For Sera Jey both counts fall, and they fall to a single fact
about a single chapter. The deeper resolution is methodological: neither a
doctrinal sorting nor a bibliographical sorting can fix the root sūtra on its
own, because a doctrinal sorting can be defeated by bibliography — as the
seventeen is — and a bibliographical sorting can be defeated by the same
bibliography, as the nine is. What survives is a criterion drawn from the
commentator rather than from the shelf, which is what p5 supplies.

## 6 · Key Terminology & Technical Concepts

- **ཡུམ། / སྲས།** — Mother and Son, as a classification of Prajñāpāramitā
  scriptures.
  - **How understood here:** the terms are *defined by a test*, not by size or
    prestige: a Mother sets out the subject-matter
    མངོན་པར་རྟོགས་པ་བརྒྱད་དུ་ཕྱེ་ནས, divided into the eight clear realizations;
    a Son shows བརྗོད་བྱ་ཕྱོགས་རེ་བ་ཙམ, only some one portion. The paragraph
    does not reject this criterion. It rejects the enumeration built on it, and
    p5 will keep the word ཡུམ while cutting the list to three.
  - **Debates:** Tsongkhapa's endorsement of the objection at
    [q02](quote:p4-q02) says both the *sense* (དོན) and the *number* are wrong,
    which is a stronger claim than p4 makes — p4 attacks only the number.

- **གྲངས་ངེས།** — a definite enumeration, a closed count.
  - **How understood here:** the target of the whole paragraph. Note that what
    is refuted is not the claim that these seventeen are Prajñāpāramitā sūtras
    — they are — but the claim that they are *exactly* the Prajñāpāramitā
    sūtras. A གྲངས་ངེས fails from either side, and p4 attacks from both.

- **མདོ་སྡུད་པ། / སྡུད་འགྲེལ།** — the *Saṃcayagāthā* and its commentary.
  - **Sanskrit equivalent:** *Ratnaguṇasaṃcayagāthā*; the commentary is
    Haribhadra's, the སྡུད་པའི་དཀའ་འགྲེལ།.
  - **How understood here:** the pivot of both refutations. Everything turns on
    whether it is a text or a chapter, and both counts placed it wrongly — the
    seventeen by making it a sixth Mother, the nine by making it the smallest of
    nine.

- **བམ་པོ། / ཤླཽ་ཀ།** — the two units of scriptural measure.
  - **How understood here:** a bam po is a fixed unit of three hundred ślokas of
    thirty-two syllables, so the two are commensurable and an argument can run
    between them — which is what the last two sentences of the paragraph do.
    The ninefold scheme names some of its members by bam po and others by
    thousands of ślokas, and that mixing is part of what makes it vulnerable.

- **ཨར་ཆེན་པོ།** — "Great Ar."
  - **How understood here:** named as the author of the ninefold scheme. On the
    standard identification this is Ar Byangchub Yeshe of Ngok Lotsāwa's
    circle, who appears in p2's lineage verse; I give that as tradition rather
    than as anything this corpus shows, since no work of his is present here.
    The title ཆེན་པོ is not ironic — the epithet stands even as the position
    falls.
